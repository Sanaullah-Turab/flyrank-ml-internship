Lane 2 — Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring

Which Pages Should a Content Reviewer Look at First?

A machine-learning study on prioritising content review queues using Google Search Console and GA4 signals from the FlyRank internship dataset.

📅 FlyRank ML Internship — 2026 📊 30,000 pages · 32 clients 🤖 Random Forest (300 trees) 🎯 Precision@K primary metric

Abstract

Question: Given a client's content inventory, which pages should a content reviewer look at first this week? Method: A client-grouped Random Forest (300 trees, max_depth=10, balanced class weights) trained on 19 numeric and 8 categorical GSC/GA4 signals extracted from a 30,000-row anonymized starter sample covering 32 pseudonymized clients, with an explicit proxy label (is_declining_label = impression trend direction = "down"). Result: The honest client-grouped model achieves Precision@50 = 0.56 and ROC-AUC = 0.66 on held-out clients, compared to a base rate of approximately 0.48 — a real but modest lift. Key finding: A naive row-level random split inflates P@50 from 0.56 to 0.92 (+0.36), because the model partly learns to recognize a client's measurement environment rather than a generalizable page-level pattern; reporting the grouped number is the honest design. Output: A ranked action queue assigning each page to one of four actions — protect, refresh_priority, refresh_review, or monitor — with interpretable reason codes for human review.

Introduction / Problem Statement

Content teams at digital marketing agencies face a recurring operational problem: a fixed pool of reviewer hours each week, but a large and constantly-changing backlog of pages that may need attention. Some pages are visibly declining in search impressions and clicks; others are stable or growing. Without a principled triage system, reviewers rely on ad hoc judgment or simple rules that treat every page the same.

The decision this work supports is: how does a reviewer with a fixed number of hours allocate their time across a large content backlog?

A content reviewer or client-side SEO owner opens the top of the ranked queue, reads the reason code(s) attached to each page, and takes one of four actions:

  • protect — top-performing page; do not touch
  • refresh_priority — two or more risk signals present; review immediately
  • refresh_review — one risk signal present; schedule a review
  • monitor — no risk signals; watch passively

Why Precision@K?

Review capacity is fixed. A false positive (flagging a healthy page) wastes a few hours — the reviewer notices quickly and moves on. A false negative (missing a real declining page) lets it keep losing impressions until the next scoring pass. Precision@K measures whether the top of the queue holds real candidates, which is the right metric for a resource-constrained review workflow. Recall on the declining set is watched as a secondary guard so high-K precision does not starve lower-ranked problem pages entirely.

Data

Release used

The anonymized starter sample: content_refresh_anonymized.csv — 30,000 rows, 44 columns, 32 pseudonymized clients. All metrics are aggregated over a trailing 90-day window. The full warehouse release (FlyRank/internship-warehouse on Hugging Face, ~79M daily rows) was explored in the data-contract notebook (w03_data_contract.ipynb); the model and validated numbers in this paper are built on the starter sample. Row filters applied

  • impressions_90d > 0 — page has real search visibility to score
  • content_age_days >= 90 — page old enough for the 90-day window to be meaningful
  • De-duplicated on content_id

All three filters were applied consistently across Weeks 5 through 7 and this capstone.

is_declining_label = (trend_direction == "down"). This is an explicitly named proxy, not an observed future outcome. trend_direction describes what already happened in the current 90-day window, not what happens next. Base rate: 54.2% of scored pages are labelled declining.

Methodology

3.1 Baseline rule (Week 4)

Rule: a page is worth a refresh review if it is visible (impressions_90d >= 500), stale (days_since_last_update >= 90), and its CTR underperforms its own position-tier-weighted baseline. Signal audits (Week 4) confirmed CTR falls in step with position, so a flat global threshold would mislabel every low-position page as broken; tier-weighted baselines are computed from training clients only and mapped to test clients at evaluation time.

Reason code: stale_ctr_underperformer. Score: impressions_90d on triggered rows — no fitted parameters.

3.2 Model (Week 5)

The ranking problem is decomposed into a classification sub-task: predict P(declining), then rank pages by that probability.

Models evaluated (simplest to strongest):

  • Logistic Regression — linear, readable coefficients, the minimum learned bar
  • Random Forest — 300 trees, max_depth=10, class_weight="balanced", random_state=42

Gradient Boosting was considered and skipped: with ~25k training rows and 18 features, Random Forest was unlikely to be meaningfully beaten, and the honest stopping point is not adding complexity once RF already clears the baseline.

3.4 Validation design — the central methodological finding

The dataset has 32 pseudonymized clients. Pages from the same client share a measurement environment: GSC setup, content strategy, industry vertical. A row-level random split lets the model see multiple pages from every client in both train and test, so it can partly learn to recognize a client rather than a generalizable page-level pattern.

This is the capstone's central finding: before/after, same model, same data, only the split design changes.

Split DesignP@50What it measures
Naive row-level random (stratified) 0.92 Inflated — partly the model recognizing a client it has already seen in training
Client-grouped holdout (~20% of clients held out entirely) 0.56 Honest — pages from clients never seen during training
Honesty gap: 0.36 points at P@50. This gap is not noise — it is the share of the random-split score that reflected client-level memorization rather than a page-level pattern that transfers to a brand the model has never met. The client-grouped number (0.56) is the figure this paper reports as the model's real performance.

3.5 Leakage audit (Week 6)

A deliberately leaky feature (trend_pct, the column the label is derived from) was injected into the same pipeline as a test of the detection harness — it pushed the score toward near-perfect 1.0, confirming the harness correctly detects leakage when present. The real feature set was then run through the full attack checklist:

  • No label-derived columns in features
  • No product-flag columns
  • Client-grouped split
  • Base rate reported alongside every number
  • Identity keys (content_id, client_id) never used as features
  • Out-of-fold metrics only

All items passed.

What the model leans on

Top-3 features by permutation importance (Week 5): traffic and engagement signals dominate. Notably, trend_pct and trend_direction do not appear in the ranked list — confirming no leakage reached the importance ranking.

Where it fails

  • False positives cluster among good-position pages with ambiguous CTR that the model cannot distinguish from pages with a genuinely branded/navigational query mix.
  • High-impact false negatives concentrate in the deep position tier, where the traffic signal is thin and the model's probability sits near the 0.5 threshold — uncertain, not confidently wrong.
  • Pages with missing word_count (mostly one content type) are systematically under-scored; the has_word_count flag alone does not fully recover that signal.

Limitations & Honest Framing

Every claim in this paper uses observed / measured / directional / decision-support language deliberately, not causal or predictive language. The model does not claim to predict future performance; it ranks pages by current risk signals to support a human decision.

The label is a proxy, not a future outcome

is_declining_label is derived from trend_direction, itself computed from the current 90-day window — it describes what already happened, not what happens next. A page labelled "declining" may already be recovering; a page labelled "stable" may be about to decline. The real next step for this lane is a genuine future-window label (prior 90 days predicting the next 30), which is not available in this dataset's fixed snapshot design.

Starter sample scope

The 30,000-row starter sample is a fixed anonymized snapshot of 32 clients at a single point in time. It does not capture seasonal variation, longitudinal trends across clients, or the full diversity of the ~79M-row warehouse panel. Findings are directional for this slice, not universal claims about search content performance.

Client-grouped honesty has a cost — and that is the point

The 0.36-point gap between the naive random-split P@50 (0.92) and the honest client-grouped P@50 (0.56) means part of the apparent model skill on a client already seen does not transfer to a client the model has never met. This is expected behaviour for a dataset with strong client-level correlations and a relatively small number of clients (32). A team deploying this model on a new client should treat early scores as directional, not validated.

Confounding and causation

The signals used (impressions, CTR, engagement rate, content freshness) are associated with the declining label in this sample, but the model is cross-sectional, not causal. Nothing here claims that refreshing a flagged page will fix it — only that the page looks worth a reviewer's attention first. No claims are made about Google's algorithm, refresh impact, or causal mechanisms.

Value-at-risk proxy

The action playbook reports a value_at_risk_usd figure of $26,611 computed as clicks_90d * cpc. This is a proxy for potential revenue at risk, not a measured revenue figure. CPC is a keyword-level estimate, and not all clicks convert. This number is provided for prioritisation context only.

Reason codes

  • stale_ctr_underperformer — Visible page (impressions above floor), stale (90+ days since last update), CTR below its position-tier baseline. The Week-4 rule signal.
  • high_decline_risk — Model-predicted decline probability ≥ 0.6 from the client-grouped Random Forest.
  • deep_thin_content — Page ranked in the deep position tier (> position 50) with low word count relative to its tier. Thin content at low visibility.
  • mature_never_refreshed — Content age > 365 days with no recorded update. Long-lived pages with no maintenance signal.
  • stable_top_performer — Protect signal: top-3/page-1 position tier, low decline risk, real traffic. Not a refresh signal.

Intended use and no-go list

This queue is a triage input for a human reviewer, not an auto-actioning system.

Intended use: Weekly or biweekly review triage. A content lead opens the refresh_priority bucket, reads the reason codes, and decides whether to schedule a refresh or investigate further.

No-go list:

  • Do not auto-publish or auto-delete content based on queue scores
  • Do not delete or deprioritise monitor rows without human review
  • Do not share raw client scores across clients or expose them externally
  • Do not interpret the priority score as a revenue guarantee
  • Do not use this model to make claims about Google's algorithm
